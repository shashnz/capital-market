Objective of this review

This e-portfolio critically reviews the article and links it to real-world Islamic Capital Market (ICM) practice. We illustrate how Ata Plus structures its Shariah-based equity crowdfunding, compare the article's regulatory details with current Securities Commission Malaysia (SC) data, map the institutions involved, and propose practical recommendations.

Why the article matters to the ICM today

The ICM is large and still growing: it reached RM2,747.79 billion at end-2025, or 63.70% of the Malaysian capital market (Securities Commission Malaysia [SC], 2026, p. 249). Yet small and medium enterprises (SMEs) still struggle to raise early-stage finance from banks. Equity crowdfunding (ECF) is regulated by the SC as a capital market channel for start-ups and SMEs (SC, 2024, Q1-Q2). The article shows how a Shariah-based ECF platform actually works in Malaysia.

Problem statement

Crowdfunding is well researched, but little is known about how Islamic equity crowdfunding operates in practice. The authors point to limited literature on Shariah-compliant crowdfunding in Malaysia, and ask: what is the operational process at Ata Plus? (Ramli et al., 2025, pp. 33-34).

Why it matters:

  • Issuers (SMEs): need to know how funding is raised and what Shariah and regulatory requirements apply.
  • Investors: need confidence that the platform is Shariah-compliant and their funds are safe.
  • Regulators: need evidence of gaps, for example Shariah governance, to improve guidelines.

Main Points and Arguments of the Article

Members 2 and 3

Contribution to the field (Member 2)

  • New insight: shows how a mudharabah-based, equity ECF platform runs in practice. Earlier work on Islamic ECF was mostly theoretical.
  • Governance finding: Ata Plus has no Shariah advisor or supervisory board, so it is better described as Shariah-friendly than fully Shariah-compliant (Ramli et al., 2025, pp. 49, 51).
  • Advances knowledge: documents the full user journey (listing, funding, closing), fees and investor rules in one place.

Methodology and findings (Member 2)

What the authors did

  • Qualitative, single case study of Ata Plus
  • Document analysis (disclosure statement, application form, terms and conditions, SC guidelines)
  • Semi-structured interviews with two Ata Plus executives (60-120 minutes) in Kuala Lumpur
  • Thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke's six phases), four themes: background, operation, regulation and governance, application guidelines (pp. 40-41)

Key findings

  • Founded March 2016; contributions from as low as RM10 (pp. 38, 41)
  • Uses mudharabah, with the platform as agent under wakalah (pp. 43-44)
  • Fees: MYR 3,000 application screening and 7.5% success fee (p. 44)
  • Regulated by the SC as a recognised market operator (p. 45)
  • No Shariah advisor; follows the SC's two-tier Shariah screening, including the 33% debt ratio benchmark (p. 49)

Our evaluation

Appropriate: a case study suits an under-researched topic where "how it works" is the question. Interviewing platform executives gives first-hand operational detail.

Weaker points: only two interviewees, both from the platform, so no investor or issuer voices; no quantitative data on campaigns, success rates or returns; much of the operational detail refers to 2016-2018.

Limitations (authors and ours): a single platform; time constraints and non-response to interview invitations (p. 52); descriptive only, so findings cannot be generalised. The authors suggest comparing other platforms or countries and adding quantitative methods.

Policy and practical implications (Member 3)

  • For the SC: issue specific guidelines for Shariah-compliant crowdfunding platforms (p. 51).
  • For Ata Plus: establish a Shariah supervisory committee to protect investor confidence and reduce reputational risk (p. 51); consider Shariah-compliant debt-based products as well (p. 51).
  • Real-world use: issuers and investors get a clear picture of the process, eligibility limits and costs before joining.

Task A: Structure of the Investment

Member 4  |  Ata Plus equity crowdfunding using Mudharabah (مضاربة) and Wakalah (وكالة). Click a step, or press Play, to see it light up on the diagram.

Figure 1. Own diagram, based on Ramli et al. (2025, pp. 42-45, 50) and SC (2024, Q7, Q13, Q18). The step order follows the article's text; the trust-account flows (steps 3, 4, 6, 7) follow the SC's trust-account requirement.

  1. Apply and screening. The entrepreneur (mudharib) registers on Ata Plus and pitches the business. The platform screens the issuer with background checks and the SC's two-tier Shariah screening (business activity, then a 33% debt ratio benchmark). Screening costs about MYR 3,000 on the full service (pp. 44, 49).
  2. Campaign is listed. Ata Plus acts only as an agent (wakalah) and finds the crowd. The issuer normally has 90 days to reach the funding target (pp. 42, 44).
  3. Investors invest. Investors (rabbul mal) put in money through the platform under the Wakalah agreement and the Master Mudharabah Crowdfunding Agreement (MMCA), which sets the profit and loss sharing ratio. They get a six-day cooling-off period (p. 44). The minimum is as low as RM10 (p. 41). Funds sit in a trust account (p. 45).
  4. Funds released. If the target is met, funds go to the issuer. If the minimum is not met in time, the money is refunded (p. 44; SC, 2024, Q13).
  5. Business runs. The mudharib uses the capital for the project and updates investors on progress online (p. 43). Ata Plus handles the legal documents and share transfer on closing, within about 14 working days (p. 50).
  6. Profit generated and repaid. Profit is shared based on the agreed ratio, and returns flow back via the trust account. The platform earns a 7.5% success fee on a successful campaign (p. 44).
  7. Return to investors. Investors receive their principal plus their share of profit. In mudharabah, losses are borne by the investor unless caused by the mudharib's misconduct, negligence or breach of terms (p. 45).
Observation. The article calls the arrangement mudharabah, but also describes shares being issued to investors on closing (pp. 42, 50). This mixes a profit-sharing contract with share ownership. We show the structure as described, and note it as a point to clarify with the lecturer.

Task B: Data Interpretation

Member 5  |  The article has no statistical tables, so we use its investor and issuer limits (pp. 46-48) as the data to interpret, and compare them with the SC's current FAQ and Annual Report 2025.

1. What the article's data shows

The article lists who may invest and how much. Retail investors are capped at RM5,000 per issuer and RM50,000 in 12 months, angel investors at RM500,000 in 12 months, and sophisticated investors have no cap. Issuers may raise up to RM3 million in 12 months (Ramli et al., 2025, pp. 46-48). In plain terms: the limits protect small investors from concentrating losses in one risky start-up, while wealthier investors can take larger positions. For issuers, the cap limits how fast they can scale through the platform.

Insight. Investor protection rules (retail and angel limits, cooling-off, one platform at a time) are stable. The big change is the issuer cap, now RM20 million over the issuer's lifetime against RM3 million in 12 months in the article. This suggests the ECF framework has matured and become more generous to issuers since the data the article describes, and that the article's operating details are partly dated. Linking the Article to ICM Issues, Challenges and Future Directions

Member 7

(a) The evolving state of the ICM

ECF sits in the product innovation and market access part of ICM development. The ICM stood at RM2,747.79 billion at end-2025, up 4.31% and equal to 63.70% of the Malaysian capital market, and Islamic AUM grew 11.35% to RM274.01 billion (SC, 2026, pp. 249, 252). Malaysia was the first ASEAN country with a regulatory framework for crowdfunding, approved in February 2015 (Ramli et al., 2025, p. 38). Under the SC's Malaysia Co-Investment Fund (MyCIF), more than RM357 million was invested through over 16,000 campaigns benefiting 2,280 MSMEs, with over RM1.36 billion co-invested from private investors (Kamaruddin et al., 2023, pp. 3-4, citing SC, 2021). (c) Future directions: recommendations

🧭 1. Issue ECF-specific Shariah guidelines

What: set clear Shariah governance requirements for Islamic ECF platforms (who must advise, what to disclose, how contracts are checked).

Who: SC with its Shariah Advisory Council (Task C, groups 1 and 2); standards guided by AAOIFI/IIFM.

Expected outcome: consistent practice across platforms and stronger investor confidence.

🕌 2. Appoint a registered Shariah adviser or committee

What: Ata Plus appoints a Shariah adviser or supervisory committee and publishes its Shariah contracts and reviews.

Who: Ata Plus, using one of the 128 SC-registered Shariah advisers (Task C, groups 2 and 4).

Expected outcome: full Shariah-compliant status, lower reputational risk, higher trust.

🔍 3. Build trust through disclosure and education

What: publish campaign results and platform reports, and run education programmes for SMEs and investors.

Who: Ata Plus and other ECF operators, with SC oversight (Task C, groups 1 and 4).

Expected outcome: higher adoption, supported by the finding that trust drives intention to use (Engku Hassan Ashari et al., 2026).

Conclusion and Reflection

Member 8, with input from all members

Summary of key findings

Ramli et al. (2025) show that Ata Plus runs a Shariah-based equity crowdfunding model using mudharabah and wakalah, regulated by the SC as a recognised market operator, with clear limits for retail, angel and sophisticated investors. The main weakness is Shariah governance: there is no Shariah advisor or supervisory board. Comparing the article with SC data shows the framework has moved on (the issuer cap is now RM20 million over the issuer's lifetime) and that the pool of registered Shariah advisers is growing, so the governance gap can be closed. We recommend ECF-specific guidelines from the SC, a Shariah adviser for Ata Plus, and stronger disclosure to build trust.
